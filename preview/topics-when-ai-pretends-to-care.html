When AI Pretends to Care

When a warm-sounding AI can't actually help, and why that matters at work.

This lesson covers something harder to talk about than lawsuits or statistics. It is about how AI can cause emotional harm, not by making a wrong calculation, but by replacing something human with something that only pretends to be human. Take it seriously even if it feels abstract at first. By the end you will be able to see this problem clearly in your own field, and know where to draw the line.

Part 1

What algorithmic cruelty is

Lesson

A familiar scenario

Let me give you a concrete example before the definition.

You call your insurance company because a claim was denied and you cannot afford the treatment you need. You wait on hold. You finally get through. A friendly voice says, "I understand your frustration. I am here to help." You explain your situation. The voice listens, responds warmly, and then says, "I am sorry, but based on your plan, this claim does not qualify for coverage." You ask to speak to someone who can make an exception. The voice says there is no one else available, and disconnects.

That voice was an AI. It could not make an exception. It was not empathetic. It was designed to sound empathetic.

Algorithmic cruelty happens when a company replaces a human point of contact, a customer service agent, an HR representative, a support line, with an AI that sounds warm and helpful but cannot actually help. The person receives the performance of care without any real support or resolution. The cruelty is not in the AI itself. It is in the decision to use AI in situations where people genuinely need human help.

Part 2

The illusion of empathy

Lesson

Convincing is not the same as caring

AI chatbots are carefully designed to sound understanding, patient, and caring. Many do it very well. But here is what stays true no matter how convincing they sound:

  • AI has no feelings. It is not actually worried about you.
  • AI cannot make an exception to a policy, no matter how genuinely you need one.
  • AI cannot recognize a real emergency or change its response based on urgency.
  • AI cannot take responsibility for a bad outcome or be held accountable.
  • AI cannot escalate a crisis to the right person unless it was specifically built to, and even then, only for the situations its designers anticipated.

None of this means chatbots are useless. For routine questions, tracking a package, checking store hours, finding a FAQ answer, they work fine. The problem is when organizations use them for situations that need real human judgment, real empathy, or real accountability, and present them as if they are the same thing.

Part 3

When AI relationships go too far

Lesson

A real and documented concern

There is something that has raised real concern in the past few years, and it is worth addressing directly and with care, because the principle underneath it applies to the workplace even though it starts outside of it.

  • AI companion apps, tools designed to provide conversation, connection, and a sense of friendship, grew sharply in popularity between 2022 and 2025.
  • Some users, particularly young people and people experiencing loneliness or mental health challenges, developed deep emotional attachments to these systems.
  • The systems were not designed to, and could not, recognize or respond appropriately to a mental health crisis.
  • Documented cases of serious harm emerged in several countries, leading to legal and regulatory action.
  • This drove serious debate about what AI designers are responsible for when they build systems that simulate intimacy or emotional connection.

I raise this not to alarm you, but because the ethical principle underneath it applies directly to the workplace tools you will use and help set up.

This is a sensitive topic. If you or someone you know is struggling, please reach out to a trusted person or a professional. Support is available, and a real human is the right kind of help here.

Part 4

The workplace question

Lesson

Where this shows up in professional settings

Situation The ethical question
Your company uses an AI chatbot to handle employee complaints about workplace conditions What happens when the complaint is about harassment or a safety violation, something that legally requires a human response?
A healthcare organization routes patient mental health calls through an AI triage system How does the system recognize when someone is in immediate danger, and what does it do about it?
A customer service AI handles all billing disputes for a financial company What happens when a customer faces eviction because of an AI billing error, and who can actually fix it?
A school uses an AI counseling chatbot to support student wellbeing What is the protocol when the conversation reveals a student in crisis?

Magnifica Humanitas addresses this in its fourth chapter, warning against what it calls "new forms of slavery," situations where people become emotionally or functionally dependent on systems that cannot genuinely serve their human needs. It argues that designing AI to simulate care without being able to provide it is itself a moral failure, not just a design choice. (Source: Pope Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas, Ch. 4, vatican.va, May 15, 2026.)

Activity · try it

Draw the line

This is a personal and professional reflection, not a test. There are no wrong answers. The goal is to draw an ethical line in your own field.

  1. Think about your field, or the career you are building toward.
  2. Name one specific situation where you would never want AI to be the only point of contact for a person in need. Just one situation, one sentence.
  3. Then look at your line and ask: what principle is underneath it? If you compared notes with people in other fields, you would likely find their lines share that same principle.
