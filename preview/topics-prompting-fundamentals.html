Prompting Fundamentals

The one skill that changes everything: how to ask an AI tool for what you actually want.

Part 1

Why the same tool gives different results

Lesson

The difference is almost never the tool

Here is something that surprises nearly every student. Two people use the exact same AI tool, on the same day, for the same task. One walks away with a polished, useful result. The other walks away frustrated, sure the AI "just does not work."

The tool did not change. The prompt did.

A prompt is simply the instruction you type in. Learning to write good prompts is the most practical skill in this whole course. You do not need to code. You do not need to understand the math. You need to communicate clearly, and you already do that every day.

Lesson

Better input, better output

Look at these two requests for the same job.

Weak prompt Strong prompt
"Write me an email." "You are a medical office coordinator. Write a 3-sentence email reminding a patient of their 2 PM appointment tomorrow. Warm, professional tone. Ask them to call if they need to reschedule."

Same tool. Wildly different results. When you give the AI more to work with, more context and a clear job, it has more to draw on. That is the whole lesson in one line.

Part 2

The RTCF framework

Lesson

Four parts of a strong prompt

Almost any solid workplace prompt has four pieces. I remember them as RTCF.

  • R is for Role. Tell the AI who to be. "You are a patient scheduler." "You are a warehouse shift lead."
  • T is for Task. Say exactly what you want done. "Write a 3-sentence reminder."
  • C is for Context. Add the details that matter. The appointment time, the tone, the audience.
  • F is for Format. Say how you want it back. A short email, a bulleted list, a table.

Think of RTCF as a quick checklist you run before you hit send on anything important.

Lesson

You do not always need all four

For a quick, low-stakes question, a short prompt is fine. Save the full RTCF checklist for anything you would put in front of a supervisor, send to a client, or use with a patient.

A complete RTCF prompt is usually just 2 to 5 sentences. The few extra seconds you spend building it will save you far more time in rewrites later.

Activity · try it

Build one RTCF prompt

Pick a real task from your own job, something you write often. Now build one prompt with all four parts:

  1. Role: who should the AI be?
  2. Task: what is the one thing you want?
  3. Context: what details does it need to get this right?
  4. Format: what should the answer look like?

Type it into ChatGPT or Gemini and read what comes back. Keep this prompt. You can reuse it any time.

Part 3

Treat it like a conversation

Lesson

Not a search engine

Most of us were trained to use technology like a search box: type, get a result, move on. AI tools work better as a back-and-forth. Treating them like a search engine is one of the biggest reasons people get stuck.

After the first answer, you can steer it with plain language:

  • "Make it shorter."
  • "Warmer tone, please."
  • "Now turn that into a bulleted list."
  • "That is close. Change the second point to focus on cost."

You do not need the perfect prompt on the first try. Start with a solid RTCF prompt, then refine. The people who get the most out of AI treat it like a working session, not a vending machine.

Lesson

The think-step-by-step nudge

Here is a simple trick that improves answers on anything with a few moving parts. Add one phrase to the end of your prompt:

"Think through this step by step before you answer."

When the model works through its reasoning out loud, it catches its own mistakes and gives you a more careful answer. It is the AI version of showing your work.
