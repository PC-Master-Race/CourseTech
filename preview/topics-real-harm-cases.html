Five real, documented cases where AI systems harmed real people, and the lesson in each.

I want to be direct with you before we start: everything in this lesson is real. These are not thought experiments or worst-case guesses. They are documented events, lawsuits that were filed, investigations that happened, and people who were harmed by AI systems used in their fields. As you read, keep asking one question about your own work: could something like this happen where I am?

Part 1

Healthcare and insurance

Lesson

An AI overruled doctors

A large health insurance company used an AI tool called nH Predict to decide how many days patients could stay in a nursing home after surgery.

  • The AI overruled the recommendations of actual doctors in thousands of cases.
  • Elderly patients were sent home too early, before they were medically ready.
  • Some suffered serious complications as a result.
  • A federal lawsuit was filed. As of 2025, legal proceedings were ongoing.

The lesson: AI must never make the final decision about someone's health, especially when a qualified professional has already reviewed the case. An algorithm does not know your patient. A doctor does.

Part 2

Real estate and business

Lesson

An AI that coordinated illegal rent increases

Competing landlords across several cities used a shared AI platform called RealPage to set rental prices.

  • The system analyzed data from all of them together and recommended coordinated price increases.
  • Agreeing on prices with your competitors is illegal under antitrust law when humans do it.
  • Because AI was doing it, some companies argued they were not breaking the law. Courts disagreed.
  • The U.S. Department of Justice filed a major antitrust case, and a proposed settlement was announced in late 2025.

The lesson: AI can be used to break the law, even when no single person intended to. "The algorithm did it" is not a legal defense. If the outcome would be illegal for humans to agree to, it is still illegal when AI coordinates it.

Part 3

Education

Lesson

Wrongly accusing students of cheating

Schools began using AI tools to detect whether students had used AI to write their assignments.

  • These tools have repeatedly been shown to produce false positives, accusing students who did not cheat.
  • Research has documented that students who speak English as a second language, and neurodivergent students, are flagged far more often than others.
  • Students were accused of dishonesty on work they genuinely wrote themselves.
  • Some faced serious consequences, grade penalties and disciplinary action, based on a tool that was wrong.

The lesson: AI tools that judge people are only as fair as the data they were trained on. If that data does not reflect the full range of human diversity, including different writing styles, languages, and ways of thinking, the tool will produce unfair results. Fairness has to be designed in. It does not happen automatically.

Part 4

Creative work and small business

Lesson

Trained on your work without permission

AI image and text models were trained on millions of photos, product descriptions, and creative works pulled from the internet.

  • This included content from small business websites, independent artists, florists, photographers, and writers.
  • Most creators were never asked for permission and never compensated.
  • Their work was used to train systems that now compete with them commercially.
  • Lawsuits from artists, authors, and news organizations are currently active in U.S. courts.

The lesson: if AI learned from your work without your knowledge or permission, you have a legitimate ethical and legal concern. This is not a fringe issue. It is one of the most active legal debates in the AI industry right now.

Part 5

Every field: invented records

Lesson

AI scribes that invent medical history

AI transcription tools called AI scribes are now widely used in clinics and hospitals to record patient conversations.

  • These tools have been documented inventing details the patient never said, adding symptoms, medications, and conditions that were never mentioned.
  • Those fabrications get saved into the patient's permanent medical record.
  • Future doctors, nurses, and specialists read that record and make decisions based on it.
  • A patient might receive the wrong treatment, or be denied the right one, because of information an AI invented.

The lesson: any AI output that affects a real person's record, finances, or health must be reviewed by a human before it is saved or acted on. This applies to every field, not just healthcare. If AI is writing something that goes into a record, a report, or a recommendation, a human needs to read it before it becomes official.

Part 6

What the cases share, and a skill to build

Lesson

The pattern across all five

Before the activity, notice what all five cases have in common.

What happened Why it mattered
An AI made a decision or output that affected a real person The harm was not hypothetical. It was concrete and documented.
No human reviewed the decision before it happened The missing human check is at the center of every case.
The people harmed often had little way to challenge the outcome The accountability gap showed up in every single one.
The AI was not malfunctioning. It was doing what it was designed to do The problem was in the design, the data, or how the tool was used, not a simple error.

Activity · try it

Lateral reading: verify one case

You should never just take someone's word for a claim about AI, including mine. Lateral reading is a skill journalists and researchers use: instead of only reading the article in front of you, you open new tabs and check whether other credible sources confirm it. Let's practice.

  1. Pick the case above that is closest to your field.
  2. Search for it using the key terms, for example "UnitedHealth nH Predict lawsuit" or "RealPage antitrust DOJ."
  3. Find one credible source that confirms it is real. Credible means a news organization, a government website (.gov), or an academic institution, not a blog or social media post.
  4. Write down three things: the name of the source, one sentence on why you trust it, and one detail from that source that was not mentioned here.

Why this matters: AI will be used to spread misinformation as easily as it is used for good. The habit of checking claims before believing them, including claims made in a lesson like this one, is one of the most valuable skills you can build. If you can verify a source, you can verify anything.

Go further. Take the case closest to your field and write the one safeguard that would have prevented it, specific enough that someone could actually put it in place. Then ask the harder question: does anything like that safeguard exist where you work right now? If not, you have found something worth raising.
