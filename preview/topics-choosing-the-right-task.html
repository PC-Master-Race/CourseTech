Choosing the Right Task for AI

A practical filter for deciding which tasks to hand to AI and which to keep for yourself.

Knowing what AI can do is one thing. Knowing which of your tasks are actually worth handing to it is the skill that separates people who get real value from people who just poke at it. In this lesson I will give you a four-question filter you can run on any task in under 30 seconds, show you what high-fit and low-fit tasks look like in your field, and teach you the one ratio that tells you whether a task is worth the effort at all. By the end you will be able to look at your own week and know exactly where AI belongs.

Part 1

Why this matters

This lesson builds on "Spotting AI Opportunities at Work." Think of it as the sharper, more practical version: a filter you can actually run in your head before you open a single tool.

Lesson

Not every task that AI can do should go to AI

Knowing the five categories of AI is a great start. But the next skill, the one that separates people who get real, lasting value from AI, is knowing which tasks to hand off and which to keep for yourself.

Here is the thing: not every task that AI can do should go to AI. This lesson gives you a practical filter you can apply to any task in under 30 seconds. By the end you will have:

  • A four-question filter for sizing up any task before you hand it to AI.
  • A clear picture of which tasks in your field are high-fit and which are low-fit.
  • An honest sense of when AI saves time, and when it just adds steps.

Part 2

The four-question task filter

Before you open any AI tool, run the task through these four questions. The more "yes" answers, the better the fit.

Lesson

Four questions, thirty seconds

# Ask yourself Yes, strong candidate for AI No, proceed with caution
1 Is it repetitive? Does it come up daily, weekly, or monthly? Weekly status updates, monthly client reports, standard follow-up emails, meeting summaries A one-time letter for a unique situation, a task you will never do again
2 Is it language or data-based? Does it involve writing, summarizing, or working with numbers? Drafting emails or proposals, summarizing long documents, interpreting a spreadsheet Physical or hands-on tasks, licensed professional judgment, work where your unique voice is the whole value
3 Is a mistake recoverable? If AI gets it wrong and you miss it, what happens? A first draft you review before sending, a summary for your own reference, a brainstorm list you evaluate Medical or legal documentation, financial output going straight to a client, anything used without a human review step
4 Is there a clear "good enough"? Is there an acceptable quality level short of perfect? Internal meeting notes, social captions you will edit, idea lists to react to Certified financial statements, legal contracts, anything where "close enough" creates liability

Quick rule of thumb: if a task takes you more than 10 minutes by hand and you will do it more than once, it is worth experimenting with AI. As your prompting skill grows, that threshold drops.

Part 3

High-fit vs. low-fit tasks by field

The same kind of task can be right for AI in one context and wrong in another. Find your field and notice where the line falls.

Lesson

Where the line falls in your field

Your field High-fit, great for AI Low-fit, keep the human in charge
Accounting / bookkeeping Plain-English summaries of monthly expenses; emails explaining billing changes; summarizing a P&L for a non-accountant Signing off on a tax filing; advice on a complex deduction; recommendations that carry professional liability
Real estate Writing a listing from bullet notes; follow-up emails after showings; summarizing a market report Determining fair market value; advising on contract terms or legal clauses
Floristry / small business Thank-you notes, captions, promotional emails; vendor inquiries; product descriptions Designing a custom arrangement; setting pricing strategy for a custom event
Education / training Summarizing a policy into talking points; drafting quiz questions from your notes; newsletters and announcements Evaluating an individual student; making a placement, accommodation, or disciplinary decision
Business owner / entrepreneur Pitch-deck outlines, website copy, vendor emails; job postings and FAQs; summarizing customer feedback Finalizing contracts; final hiring decisions; setting pricing strategy
Healthcare / social services Summarizing a recorded meeting for internal notes; a general FAQ for clients; internal staff communications Clinical documentation, diagnosis, or treatment planning; any client-facing output used without professional sign-off
Software / tech Boilerplate code, documentation drafts, test-case outlines; user stories; release notes Security-critical code review; architecture decisions; production deployment or incident response

Part 4

The effort-to-value ratio

Here is the part most people miss. Just because AI can do something does not mean you should ask it to.

Lesson

Will the time saved beat the time spent?

There is always a setup cost with AI: writing the prompt, reviewing the output, fixing errors, formatting the result. So before every AI task, ask yourself one question:

"Will the time I save be greater than the time it takes me to write a good prompt, review the output, and fix what needs fixing?"

If yes, hand it to AI. If no, just do it yourself and move on. Here is a quick gut-check:

AI is likely worth it when... AI may not be worth it when...
The task takes more than 10 minutes by hand The task takes 2 to 3 minutes and needs no real thinking
You will do this same task again, regularly It is a one-time task you will never repeat
The output is a starting point you will review and edit The output must be perfect, with zero tolerance for error
The format is repeatable (emails, summaries, reports, lists) Writing the prompt and checking the output would take longer than just doing the task

AI is a force multiplier, not a magic shortcut. Used strategically, it hands you back hours a week. Used carelessly, it adds steps to tasks that were already simple.

Part 5

Put it to work

Activity · try it

Audit your own trade

By the end of this, you will have run three of your own tasks through the filter and produced one piece of real, field-specific AI output.

Step 1, list three tasks. Think about your typical work week. Write down three specific tasks that involve writing, communication, or information. Be specific. Not "write emails," but "write follow-up emails to clients after a property showing."

Step 2, run the filter. For each task, answer yes or no to the four questions. The task with the most yes answers is your highest-fit task. That is the one you will use next.

Step 3, prompt with RTCF. Open ChatGPT or Gemini and build a prompt with Role, Task, Context, and Format. Use this scaffold, filling in the brackets:

"You are a [your job title]. Write a [specific deliverable] for [who it is for]. Here is the background you need: [key details]. Format it as [length, tone, structure, for example 3 short bullet points in plain language]."

Step 4, reflect. Run the prompt and read the output. Then answer, in a sentence or two each: How close was it to what you actually needed? What would you change in the prompt to get a better result? Would you use this at work with your own edits, and why or why not?

Finished early? Run the same prompt through both ChatGPT and Gemini and compare. Which is closer to your voice? The comparison itself is the lesson.

Activity · try it

Start your AI Workplace Framework

This is a living document you can keep building over time. Open a blank doc and start three sections:

  1. Your role. A sentence or two on what you do.
  2. Your top 3 AI-ready tasks. Pull these from the audit above, the ones that passed the filter.
  3. Your best prompt from this lesson. Paste in the RTCF prompt that gave you the most useful result.

Keep this document. As you learn more, you can add a risk plan for each task ("what could go wrong, and how would I catch it?") and turn a simple capability list into a responsible plan for using AI in your work.

Lesson

Dig deeper: recommended reading

Each of these takes 5 to 15 minutes.

  • McKinsey Global Institute, "Generative AI and the Future of Work." Which tasks are most automatable, and why.
  • Harvard Business Review, "How to Use AI Tools Effectively at Work." Practical, task-level advice.
  • MIT Sloan Management Review, "When Should You Trust AI, and When Should You Not?" A clear framework for the judgment call.
  • Pew Research Center, "AI in the Workplace." How workers are actually experiencing AI on the job.
  • Brookings Institution, "How Will AI Affect Jobs?" A framework for thinking about task-level impact.
