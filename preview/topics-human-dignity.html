Human Dignity and the Right to an Explanation

Treating people as people, not data points, and your emerging right to know why AI decided.

The earlier ethics lessons looked at what goes wrong. This one asks the opposite question: what should go right? What does it actually mean to build and use AI in a way that treats every person as a full human being? I will start with something concrete before the big ideas, so the ideas already make sense when we get to them.

Part 1

What human dignity means in plain terms

Lesson

Being treated as a person, not a number

Human dignity is not a complicated idea. It means this: every person deserves to be treated as a full human being, not as a number, not as a data point, not as a risk category, and not as a productivity score.

Think about a time you called a company and felt like a case number instead of a person. Or a time a system made a decision about you and no one explained why. Or a time you felt like the process did not see you at all, just your record. That feeling has a name. It is a violation of dignity. And AI systems, designed without care, can cause that feeling at a massive scale, to thousands of people at once, without anyone intending to.

The core idea: AI systems that reduce people to numbers cause real harm even when no individual intended it. The harm does not require bad intentions. It requires only a system that was never designed to see the person behind the data.

Part 2

Magnifica Humanitas: what it is

Lesson

A moral argument addressed to everyone

On May 15, 2026, Pope Leo XIV published a document called Magnifica Humanitas, "The Magnificent Humanity." It is a papal encyclical, one of the most significant documents a Pope can issue, and this one was written specifically about artificial intelligence and the human person.

To be very clear: this document is addressed to all people, regardless of faith or background. It is not a religious argument in the narrow sense. It is a moral argument about what we owe each other when we build and use technology that affects people's lives. You do not need to agree with everything in it, and you do not need to share any particular faith. You only need to engage with the ideas seriously, because they are grounded in principles that show up in law, policy, and workplace practice.

At a glance: published May 15, 2026, by Pope Leo XIV; full title Magnifica Humanitas: On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence; official source vatican.va; audience, all people; core argument, AI is a powerful tool, but it must be designed and used in ways that protect the dignity, equality, and accountability every person deserves.

Lesson

What it actually says, chapter by chapter

Here are the four chapters most relevant to this course, in plain language.

Chapter What it is about How it connects
Ch. 2, Equal dignity of all human beings Every person has equal worth, regardless of where they were born, what language they speak, or what they look like. No system, including AI, can treat people as less worthy on those grounds. Ties directly to AI bias, predictive redlining, and the Amazon hiring tool.
Ch. 3, Responsibility, transparency, and governance AI is valuable but must never decide people's lives, employment, health, freedom, without transparent human oversight. A machine that decides your fate with no explanation violates your dignity. Ties directly to the accountability gap and the black box problem.
Ch. 4, Dignity of work and breaking new forms of dependency People must not become emotionally or functionally dependent on systems that cannot genuinely serve their needs. Designing AI to simulate care it cannot provide is a moral failure. Ties directly to algorithmic cruelty and AI companion systems.
Ch. 5, AI and weapons AI must never be given the authority to decide to use lethal force without a human making that choice. Extends the accountability gap to its most serious consequence: life-and-death decisions made by machines.

Full citation: Pope Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas: On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence, Encyclical Letter, Vatican City, May 15, 2026.

Part 3

The right to an explanation

Lesson

You may already have this right

Here is something most people do not know: in many parts of the world, you now have, or are gaining, the legal right to know why an AI system made a decision about you.

Where What the law says Status
European Union The EU AI Act gives people the right to an explanation when an AI system makes a significant decision about them, a loan denial, a job rejection, a medical determination. In effect as of 2025. Companies must comply.
United States No comprehensive federal right to explanation exists yet. Some states have passed limited protections, and Congress is actively debating broader legislation. Ongoing. Rules vary by state and industry.
Your workplace Many organizations are now writing internal AI policies that include disclosure and explanation requirements, even where no law requires it yet. Check your employee handbook or ask HR.

A question to sit with: should people in the United States have the right to know why an AI system made a decision that affected their life? And if yes, what would that actually look like in your field?

Part 4

The new Luddites

Lesson

Not anti-technology, pro-dignity

You may have heard "Luddite" used as an insult, meaning someone afraid of technology. That is not what the word originally meant.

The original Luddites were skilled craftspeople in early 19th-century England. They were not afraid of machines in general. They objected specifically to machines being used to lower wages, eliminate skilled work, and concentrate power in the hands of factory owners at the expense of workers who had spent years building expertise.

Today, a growing movement of technologists, workers, and ethicists asks the same question. Not "should we use AI?" but "is this particular use of AI making work more human, or less? Is it expanding what people can do, or replacing the dignity of skilled work with cheaper automation?"

The question worth asking in your field. For each AI tool you meet in your career, ask: is this helping me do better work, or is it being used to replace my judgment, reduce my wages, or eliminate the expertise that took me years to build? The answer is not always obvious, but asking it is the mark of a thoughtful professional.

Activity · try it

Does AI treat you as a person?

This reflection uses your own experience. You do not need an AI example, any automated system counts.

  1. Think of a time you dealt with an automated system, a chatbot, a phone menu, a screening tool, an automated rejection email, and felt like you were not being treated as a full human being.
  2. Ask yourself what specifically made it feel that way. Was it the lack of explanation? The inability to make an exception? The feeling of being categorized?
  3. Then answer the closing question: what would it actually look like for an AI tool in your workplace to respect the dignity of every person it touches? Name one specific design choice that would make a difference.
