The Hidden Costs of AI

What AI costs the world just by running, plus the knowledge problem most courses never mention.

Most conversations about AI focus on what it can do and what can go wrong with its outputs. This lesson looks at something different: what AI costs the world just by existing and running. These costs are real, already measurable, and growing. I am keeping it short on purpose. You do not need to become an environmental scientist. You need enough to ask smart questions when your organization makes decisions about AI. At the end, you will pull everything from these ethics lessons into a short plan for your own work.

Part 1

The environmental cost

Lesson

Electricity, water, and carbon

When you use ChatGPT or any AI tool, a server somewhere processes your request. That server generates enormous heat, and cooling it takes two things: electricity and water. A lot of both.

The cost Why it matters
Electricity: a single modern AI data center can use as much power as 100,000 homes, and many of the largest ones being built now are expected to use up to 20 times that. By one estimate, data centers could reach up to 12% of all U.S. electricity use by 2028. This demand is already pushing power bills up. In 2025, U.S. electricity rate increases topped $60 billion, with average prices up almost 10% from 2024.
Water: data centers need huge amounts of water to keep their servers cool. A mid-sized facility can use up to 300,000 gallons a day, and a large one up to 5 million, about what a small town uses. By 2028, AI-related U.S. data centers could need up to 32 billion gallons of water a year, enough for roughly 360,000 households. That water often comes from the same supplies local communities rely on. Two-thirds of U.S. data centers built or in development since 2022 sit in water-stressed areas.
Carbon and air: data centers need constant power, so many rely on gas generation for daily use and diesel generators for backup. Both release greenhouse gases and health-harming pollutants. This ties AI's growth directly to climate change, and to local air quality in communities that often already face pollution.

These figures come from the World Resources Institute, "From Energy Use to Air Quality, the Many Ways Data Centers Affect US Communities" (wri.org, 2026), which draws on the International Energy Agency, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and others.

Magnifica Humanitas touches this in its fourth chapter with the idea of "the universal destination of goods," the principle that the earth's resources belong to all people, not just those who can build the largest data centers. The communities bearing the environmental cost are often not the ones receiving the economic benefit. (Source: Pope Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas, Ch. 4, vatican.va, May 15, 2026.)

Part 2

The knowledge problem

Lesson

AI is increasingly learning from AI

Here is the second hidden cost, less visible but just as important for the future of AI and the people who use it.

When AI models were first trained, they learned from content made by human beings: books, articles, websites, research, conversations. That content reflected real human thought and experience. But now the internet is filling up with AI-generated content, and new models are increasingly learning from that, text and images produced by earlier AI.

Think of it like making a photocopy of a photocopy of a photocopy. Each generation loses a little more clarity. The output becomes blander, less accurate, and less grounded in real human experience.

Model collapse is what researchers call the gradual decline in AI output quality that happens when models are trained on AI-generated data instead of original human content. The model loses the richness, diversity, and accuracy of real human knowledge, and starts producing output that sounds plausible but holds less genuine insight.

Lesson

Who gets erased first

Here is the part that should concern you most. When AI smooths out unusual or rare patterns in its training data, it tends to erase minority perspectives, rare languages, and unconventional ideas first. The more a viewpoint differs from the majority of the data, the more likely it is to be treated as noise and filtered out.

So AI models become more fluent for the majority and less useful, or actively harmful, for everyone else. The groups who already have less representation in the data end up with even less representation in the AI outputs that affect their lives.

And there is a broader version of the deskilling idea from the bias lesson:

  • If workers hand off writing, analysis, and judgment entirely to AI, they slowly lose the ability to do those things themselves.
  • If they lose that ability, they also lose the ability to catch AI's errors, which is the one safeguard that makes human oversight meaningful.
  • If enough workers in a field lose those skills, the whole field loses its ability to function without AI, a dependency no one consciously chose.
  • This course is built to prevent that for you. Using AI as a tool that enhances your work is very different from using it as a replacement for your thinking.

One fact that surprised you

Write one sentence: the fact that surprised you most, and why. Be specific. Not "AI uses a lot of water," but something like "I did not know that a single AI data center can use as much power as 100,000 homes, and that surprises me because..."

Part 3

Capstone: your ethics layer

This pulls together everything from these ethics lessons and applies it to your own work. If you started an AI Workplace Framework in "Choosing the Right Task for AI," open it and find the AI-ready task you identified. That task is what you are examining now.

Lesson

The thread across your framework

Here is how the pieces fit together over time:

  • First, you found the opportunity, a task in your field that AI could realistically help with.
  • Now, you examine whether it is safe and ethical to pursue, and whether any part of it could cause harm to others or to you over the long term.
  • Later, you can present the full picture: the opportunity, the risks, and your plan for handling both.

Add an "ethics layer" to your task by answering the four questions below, as honestly and specifically as you can. There are no right answers, only thoughtful ones and careless ones.

Activity · try it

Add the ethics layer to your task

Answer all four for the AI task you chose.

1. Does this tool affect other people? Who else is involved, customers, coworkers, patients, students, clients, applicants, anyone beyond you? Name them specifically. Example: "My task is using AI to screen job applicants. The people affected are the applicants, who may never know an AI reviewed their resume."

2. Could it produce an unfair or harmful outcome? Is there any realistic way that bias in the data, a hallucination, or a bad output could harm someone? Even a small chance counts. If you cannot think of any risk, think harder. Every tool has one. Example: "If the AI summarizes patient notes wrong, a doctor might make a decision based on wrong information."

3. Does anyone have the right to know AI was involved? If AI helped write a document, generate an image, score an application, or make a recommendation, does the person receiving it deserve to know? What does your workplace policy say, and what do you think it should say? Example: "If AI wrote a performance review about an employee, I think that employee has the right to know."

4. What is the human backup plan? What happens if the AI gets it wrong? Who reviews the output before it affects a real person? Who is accountable if it causes harm? If you cannot answer this clearly, that is your ethics concern right there. Example: "Before any AI-generated report goes to a client, my supervisor reviews it, and we are both accountable."

When you finish, look for the single biggest ethical concern in your answers. That is the one to watch most closely.
