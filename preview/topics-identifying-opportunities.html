Spotting AI Opportunities at Work

Connecting AI to your actual work. Which tasks are worth handing to AI, and which still need a human.

Knowing how to use AI only helps once you know where to use it. In this lesson I will give you two quick tests for deciding which tasks are worth handing to AI and which ones still need you in the lead. That judgment is exactly what turns AI into real time saved instead of time wasted.

Part 1

From understanding AI to using it on purpose

Lesson

Where does it belong in your work?

You now know what AI is and how to talk to it. The next question, the one that actually changes your career, is this: where does it belong in your work?

Not every task is a good fit. Not every workflow needs to be touched. The professionals who get real value are not the ones who try to use AI for everything. They are the ones who build a sharp eye for which tasks AI handles well and which ones still need a person.

This topic gives you two practical tools for that eye: a four-question check, and a central question that will follow you for the rest of your career. The activity at the end puts both to work on your real job.

Part 2

The four-question suitability check

When a task lands on your plate and you wonder "could AI help here?", run it through four questions. A task does not need a yes on all four. But the more yes answers it gets, the stronger a candidate it is.

Lesson

Four questions to ask

Question What a "yes" means for you
Q1 Is it repetitive or routine? You do it often, it follows a predictable pattern, and the steps are mostly the same. High repetition means high potential. AI can draft, format, or generate standard outputs far faster than doing it by hand, freeing you for the judgment calls that need your expertise.
Q2 Is it mostly language-based? The output is text: writing, summarizing, translating, explaining, documenting, responding. If the core of the task is producing or editing written language, AI is your most powerful ally. This is where today's tools are strongest.
Q3 Is it information-heavy? It means pulling from a large body of information, comparing options, or synthesizing research that would take a person real time. AI can scan, sort, compare, and summarize large amounts of information quickly. That makes it valuable for research support and turning raw data into usable summaries.
Q4 Is it low-stakes if AI gets it wrong? You are drafting something you will review, not making a final call without oversight. The lower the stakes of an error, the more freely you can use AI as a drafting partner. High-stakes outputs like medical decisions, legal documents, or safety procedures need careful checking at every step.

A practical rule of thumb: if a task is repetitive, language-based, information-heavy, and low-stakes when imperfect, it is almost certainly worth trying with AI. If it is one of a kind, needs physical action, calls for deep ethical judgment, or has zero room for error, keep the human in the lead.

Part 3

The central question: remove, accelerate, or improve?

Once you spot a task that could be a fit, ask one more thing before you start prompting. This cuts through the noise: "What does this remove, accelerate, or improve in my workflow?"

Lesson

Three kinds of value

Each verb points to a different kind of value. Knowing which one applies helps you prompt better and helps you explain the value to a supervisor.

If AI... It means... Real examples
Removes a task entirely A whole step gets handed off, freeing your time. You stop doing it by hand. Formatting 50 meeting notes into summaries. Drafting the same reminder email 30 times a week. Generating first-draft job descriptions from a template.
Accelerates a task You still do the work, but hours become minutes. You stay in control, AI just makes you faster. Researching background for a proposal (an hour becomes 10 minutes). Writing a first draft for your supervisor to edit (two hours becomes 30 minutes). Translating a document for a client.
Improves a task The output gets better: more professional, more complete, better structured, more consistent. An email becomes clearer and more persuasive. A report gains a logical structure. A customer message feels warmer. A complex policy gets explained more clearly.

In practice, many uses do all three at once: removing the tedious part, speeding up what remains, and improving the overall quality. But naming the main goal helps you prompt more effectively and judge the output more honestly.

Part 4

Same tool, different trades

AI language tools are generalists. The same ChatGPT or Gemini that helps a marketing manager write ad copy will help a warehouse supervisor write a shift handoff report, if it is given the right context. What changes across trades is not the tool. It is the prompts and the use cases.

Lesson

How it shows up across fields

Field Remove Accelerate Improve
Healthcare & medical office Writing routine reminders and confirmations from scratch each time Summarizing intake notes; researching medication interactions for a nurse Patient-facing messages; documentation clarity; staff training materials
Warehouse & logistics Drafting shift handoff notes, discrepancy reports, incident summaries Analyzing shipment data for patterns; turning verbal descriptions into standard procedures Safety briefings; vendor communication; performance write-ups
Customer service & retail Writing responses to common complaints; building FAQ documents Drafting escalation emails; translating for multilingual customers Tone of customer messages; consistency across the team
Business & administrative Meeting agendas and follow-up summaries; routine memos Researching vendors or competitors; analyzing survey feedback; presentation outlines Executive summaries; proposals; correspondence quality
Construction & skilled trades Project status updates, material orders, subcontractor messages Looking up code requirements; comparing product specs; generating checklists Client proposals; safety documentation; bid writing
Education support & childcare Routine parent communications and progress templates Researching age-appropriate activities; summarizing observation notes Parent newsletters; lesson summaries; draft documentation for educator review

Part 5

The flip side: where AI falls short

Knowing where AI shines is half the skill. Knowing where it struggles keeps you from handing over tasks it cannot handle well. We go deep on the risks in "When AI Gets It Wrong." Here is your quick orientation.

Lesson

Five places to keep the human in charge

Where AI struggles Why, and what to do instead
Physical judgment or hands-on skill AI works with language and data. It cannot tell if a wound looks infected, if a machine sounds off, or if a weld is sound. Physical expertise stays with you.
Current, real-time information LLMs are trained up to a cutoff date and do not browse the web unless that feature is on. For anything current, like laws, live prices, or breaking news, verify with a primary source.
Deep ethical or legal judgment AI can draft and summarize, but it cannot replace the ethical reasoning and legal responsibility that must sit with a qualified professional. Use it to prepare the thinking, not to make the call.
Confidential or sensitive data Be very careful what you enter into public AI tools. Patient information, proprietary data, personnel files, and financial records should not go into tools like ChatGPT without knowing your organization's privacy policy.
Guaranteed accuracy AI can be confidently wrong. For anything where an error carries real consequences, like a medication dose or a safety procedure, treat AI output as a draft that needs verification, not a finished product.

Part 6

Practice and go deeper

Activity · try it

Activity: baseline chat session

Purpose: to find out firsthand what AI thinks about your specific field, and to start a personal list of tasks where AI could add real value.

What you will do:

  1. Open ChatGPT (chat.openai.com) or Gemini (gemini.google.com). Log in or create a free account if you need one.
  2. Ask the AI what tasks it could help with in your exact job. Be specific about your title, not just "healthcare worker," but "medical billing specialist," "CNA," or "warehouse picker."
  3. Read the response carefully. Does it know your field? Are its ideas realistic? Do any feel off-base?
  4. Note your top 3 most useful suggestions, and flag any that seemed wrong, generic, or surprising.
  5. If you have time and feel comfortable, pick one suggestion and test it. Ask the AI to actually help with that task using your RTCF skills.

Advanced option: in the same chat, reply: "Which of these would save me the most time, and why? Then help me do that task right now using a strong prompt." That is great practice at conversational iteration.

Reflection: after seeing what AI suggested for your field, what surprised you? Was it more knowledgeable, less knowledgeable, or about what you expected? Save your chat or take a screenshot. You may want it later.

Lesson

What you can do now

After this topic and the baseline chat session, you should be able to:

  • Apply the four-question check to any task and make a sound decision about whether AI fits.
  • Use remove / accelerate / improve to name the kind of value AI can add.
  • Recognize AI opportunities specific to your field, including ones you had not considered.
  • Identify tasks where AI is not well suited, and explain why the human must stay in the lead.
  • Name at least 3 real tasks from your job where AI could save time, improve quality, or both.

Lesson

Dig deeper: recommended reading

Each of these takes 5 to 15 minutes.

  • McKinsey Global Institute, "The Economic Potential of Generative AI: The Next Productivity Frontier." Landmark research on which job tasks AI is most likely to transform, with data across dozens of fields.
  • Harvard Business Review, "How Generative AI Can Augment Human Creativity." A nuanced look at which work benefits most from AI, and which needs human-only judgment.
  • Brookings Institution, "How Artificial Intelligence Is Transforming the World." A clear, balanced overview of AI's real-world impact on industries, jobs, and communities.
  • U.S. Department of Labor, O*NET, "Occupation-Specific AI Exposure Data." The government's occupational database. Search your own job for a data-grounded view.
  • World Economic Forum, "Future of Jobs Report." A global forecast on which jobs and skills are growing, which are declining, and where AI is the driver.
