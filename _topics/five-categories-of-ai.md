- title: Why a map matters
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Knowing the categories does three things for you
    body: |
      Before this course, you may have pictured AI as one big thing, ChatGPT, or maybe just "the technology" in general. But AI at work is really a whole range of different capabilities, each designed for a different type of task. Learning the five categories pays off in three concrete ways:

      - **It helps you match the right tool to the right task**, instead of forcing everything through one tool that may not be the best fit.
      - **It helps you size up new tools quickly.** When someone shows you a new AI product, you can immediately ask, "Which category does this belong to?"
      - **It gives you the vocabulary** to describe exactly where AI fits in your own field.

      Let's go through all five, one at a time.
- title: 'Category 1: Language and Communication'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Anything built out of words
    body: |
      This is the category you have probably already met. These tools read, write, rewrite, summarize, and brainstorm in plain language.

      **Key tools:**

      - **ChatGPT:** write, rewrite, summarize, brainstorm, and draft in any tone or style.
      - **Gemini:** Google's AI, great for drafting inside Google Docs and Gmail.
      - **Microsoft Copilot:** built into Word and Outlook to rewrite, summarize, and generate from a prompt.

      | Field / role | How they use this category |
      |---|---|
      | Healthcare admin | Rewrite a patient intake letter in plain English. |
      | Marketing | Draft five subject lines for an email campaign. |
      | HR / recruiting | Summarize a job description into three bullet points for a job posting. |
      | Customer service | Generate five responses to common complaint messages. |
      | Education | Write a quiz from a lesson outline. |
- title: 'Category 2: Data and Analysis'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Ask questions of your numbers, in plain English
    body: |
      These tools let you work with spreadsheets and data without knowing a single formula. You ask a question in plain language, and the tool finds the answer.

      **Key tools:**

      - **Copilot in Excel:** ask questions about your spreadsheet in plain English, no formulas required.
      - **Julius AI:** upload a spreadsheet or dataset and chat with it to find trends and patterns.
      - **Gemini in Google Sheets:** summarize data, create charts, and answer questions right inside Sheets.

      | Field / role | How they use this category |
      |---|---|
      | Small business owner | Ask: "Which product category had the highest sales last quarter?" |
      | Nonprofit coordinator | Summarize survey responses into key themes without reading every row. |
      | Healthcare | Find patterns in patient appointment no-show data. |
      | Retail / operations | Spot inventory trends across several store locations. |
      | Finance / accounting | Flag unusual transactions using plain-language prompts. |
- title: 'Category 3: Image and Visual'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Make a picture from a description
    body: |
      These tools create original visuals from a written prompt. There is a full lesson on this later in the course, where you will make one yourself.

      **Key tools:**

      - **Canva AI (Magic Media):** generate images from text inside Canva's design platform.
      - **Gemini (Nano Banana):** Google's image model, built into Gemini. Ask it in plain language to create or change a picture. The odd name is the one Google itself uses for it.
      - **Adobe Firefly:** Adobe's AI image tool, which works inside Photoshop and Express.

      | Field / role | How they use this category |
      |---|---|
      | Marketing / social media | Generate on-brand visuals for posts without a graphic designer. |
      | Real estate | Create lifestyle images for listings or promotional flyers. |
      | Education | Illustrate concepts or generate diagrams for course materials. |
      | Event planning | Draft promotional graphics for flyers and invitations. |
      | Retail / e-commerce | Create product mock-ups or background images for listings. |
- title: 'Category 4: Audio and Voice'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: The category most beginners overlook
    body: |
      Audio AI is the category most people skip right past, and it is one of the highest-value ones. It works on your phone, in your browser, and in tools you may already use. Here is what it does:

      | What it does | In plain English |
      |---|---|
      | **Speech-to-text transcription** | You speak, the AI types. Every word turned into text automatically. No more retyping notes from a conversation you just had. |
      | **Meeting summarization** | The AI listens to a recorded or live meeting and produces a written summary: key points, action items, decisions made. |
      | **Voice note automation** | Speak a quick memo after a client visit. The AI turns it into a structured, readable note you can save or file. |
      | **Note-taking on the go** | No laptop needed. Open the app, press record, talk through what just happened, and the AI handles the write-up. |

      **Key tools:** Otter.ai (real-time transcription and meeting summaries), Whisper by OpenAI (very accurate transcription of uploaded audio), and Suno or Udio (royalty-free background music).

      | Field / role | How they use this category |
      |---|---|
      | Project manager | Transcribe a client meeting and get action items automatically. |
      | Content creator | Generate a copyright-free music track for a video or reel. |
      | Healthcare | Transcribe physician notes or intake interviews. |
      | Legal / admin | Turn recorded interviews into searchable text. |
      | Real estate | Dictate showing notes into your phone the moment you leave, and get a formatted client file, instead of typing at 10 PM. |

      **Key insight:** transcription alone saves hours a week for anyone who sits in meetings. If you spend time talking, this category is worth trying first.
- title: 'Category 5: Workflow and Automation'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Connect your tools so tasks run themselves
    body: |
      This category is less about writing one thing and more about connecting your tools so routine steps happen on their own.

      **Key tools:**

      - **Microsoft Copilot (full suite):** automate repetitive tasks across Word, Excel, Outlook, and Teams.
      - **Zapier AI:** connect apps and trigger automated workflows without writing code.
      - **Make:** build multi-step automations between tools with a visual drag-and-drop editor.

      | Field / role | How they use this category |
      |---|---|
      | Office admin | Route incoming emails into folders and draft reply templates automatically. |
      | HR | Trigger an onboarding checklist when a new hire is added to a spreadsheet. |
      | Marketing | Auto-post approved content when a file is added to a folder. |
      | Operations | Send a Slack alert whenever a form is submitted with a certain keyword. |
      | Small business | Connect your scheduling tool, payment system, and email list so they talk to each other. |

  - type: lesson
    title: A quick word on bots versus agents
    body: |
      There is one more distinction worth knowing before you go further: the difference between a bot and an AI agent. A **bot** follows fixed rules, if X happens, do Y. An **AI agent** reasons, plans, and decides what to do next.

      Most of the tools in these five categories behave more like bots right now: powerful, but rule-based. Agents are the next step, tools you hand a goal and they figure out the steps. If a tool asks you for a goal and then handles the steps itself, you are working with an agent. There are two full lessons on this: "Bots vs. AI Agents" and "Scoped vs. Autonomous Agents."
  - type: activity
    title: Category sort and field map
    body: |
      Let's make this map real for your own work.

      1. Look at your field, or the role you are working toward.
      2. Pick the **two categories** that feel most relevant to your day-to-day work.
      3. For each of those two, name **one specific task** you could hand off to an AI tool.
      4. Write each as a single sentence.

      Example: "Language and Communication. I could use AI to draft follow-up emails after client meetings."

      Then push yourself with two more questions: which category feels hardest to apply to your work right now, and why? And is any category one you had never thought to use before? The map is not the same for every field. Your job is to know *your* map.

      **Go further.** Design a small workflow that chains two categories together for a real task in your work, for example audio to capture a meeting, then language to turn it into a summary you would actually send. Write the steps in order, and mark the point where you would review the output before it goes anywhere. Combining categories is where the real time savings live.
