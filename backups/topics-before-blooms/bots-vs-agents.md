- title: What a bot actually is
  intro: Picture a vending machine. You press B4. It drops the chips in slot B4. Every time. It does not look at what else is inside, it does not wonder if you would prefer C2, and it never decides to restock itself.
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Fast, consistent, and rigid
    body: |
      A bot works the same way. It is a software program built to respond to specific triggers with specific actions, set in advance by whoever built it. Bots do not reason. They do not weigh options. They do not handle situations they were not programmed for. They react.

      > **Bot:** a software program that responds to specific triggers with pre-defined actions. Bots follow rules: if this happens, do that. They are fast, consistent, and reliable within their lane. They do not learn, reason, or adapt to situations outside their programming.

      You have already met plenty of bots:

      | Bot type | What it does, and what it cannot do |
      |---|---|
      | Website chat widget | Answers the same 10 FAQ questions. Cannot handle anything off that list, usually says "I'll connect you to an agent" when it fails. |
      | Auto-reply email | Sends a confirmation every time a form is submitted. Does not read the form or change its response. |
      | Scheduling bot | Books meetings when both calendars are free. Cannot negotiate, suggest alternatives, or understand "I prefer mornings." |
      | Social media auto-poster | Posts at a scheduled time. Does not judge whether the post fits that day's news. |
      | Payroll automation | Calculates and sends pay from logged hours. Cannot fix a missed punch-in without a human correcting the record first. |

      The honest truth: bots are not dumb, they are deliberately simple. Their value is consistency and speed. A good bot does the same task the same way a thousand times a day without fatigue or bad moods. The limit is not intelligence, it is flexibility. The moment a situation falls outside its rules, the bot fails or hands off.
- title: What an AI agent actually is
  intro: 'Now picture a different kind of helper. On Monday you say: "I need a competitive analysis of three local vendors by Thursday. Check their websites, pull pricing, note any red flags, and put it on one page." Then you walk away.'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: You give it a goal, it works the steps
    body: |
      A good human assistant could handle that. They would break the goal into steps, make decisions along the way, use several tools, and produce something useful, all without you micromanaging.

      An AI agent is built to work the same way. You give it a goal. It reasons through the steps, chooses and uses tools, handles obstacles, and works toward the outcome, often without a human approving each individual action.

      > **AI agent:** an AI system that receives a goal and plans and carries out the steps to reach it on its own. Agents can use multiple tools, make decisions mid-task, and adapt when something unexpected happens. Unlike bots, they are not limited to a pre-set script. They reason about what to do next.
  - type: lesson
    title: How an agent approaches a task
    body: |
      Here is what happens under the hood when you hand a goal to an agent. This loop is what separates agents from every simpler tool you have used.

      | Stage | What happens | Why it matters |
      |---|---|---|
      | **1. Receive goal** | You give the agent a goal in plain language. | No rules to pre-program. The agent interprets your intent. |
      | **2. Plan steps** | It breaks the goal into a sequence of sub-tasks. | Like a project plan, generated automatically. |
      | **3. Select tools** | It chooses which tools to use: web search, a document editor, a calendar, an API. | One agent can coordinate many tools in sequence. |
      | **4. Take action** | It executes each step, reviewing the result before the next. | It checks its own work as it goes. |
      | **5. Adapt** | If a step fails or returns something unexpected, it adjusts its plan. | This is what bots cannot do: recover from the unexpected. |
      | **6. Deliver output** | It returns the finished result to you. | You review the output, not every individual step. |
- title: The comparison at a glance
  intro: Here is the full picture side by side. The differences are subtle in some rows and dramatic in others.
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Bot versus agent
    body: |
      | | Bot | AI agent |
      |---|---|---|
      | **How it works** | Follows fixed rules: if X, do Y | Reasons toward a goal: plans and adapts |
      | **What you give it** | A trigger or specific input | A goal or outcome in plain language |
      | **Flexibility** | Rigid, handles only what it was built for | Flexible, adapts to new situations |
      | **Tool use** | Uses the one tool it was built around | Coordinates multiple tools in sequence |
      | **When it fails** | Stops or hands off to a human | Tries an alternative approach first |
      | **Oversight needed** | Set it up, then check outputs periodically | Review final output and critical decision points |
      | **Workplace example** | Auto-reply email when a form is submitted | Draft a summary from three data sources, format it, and email it to the team |
      | **Setup speed** | Fast, configure the rules once | More complex, needs a clear goal and tool access |
      | **Risk level** | Low, predictable behavior | Higher, can take real actions with real consequences |
- title: Why this distinction matters for you
  intro: You might think this is a developer's problem, not yours. Here is why every professional needs it, even if you never build one.
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Three reasons this is your business
    body: |
      - **You will be asked to evaluate tools.** When your organization considers a new AI tool, "is this a bot or an agent?" tells you how much autonomy it has, and how much oversight you need to keep.
      - **You will be responsible for the output.** Bots produce predictable, auditable output. Agents produce variable output from a chain of decisions you did not make. Knowing which you are working with changes how carefully you review results.
      - **Agents are coming to your field soon.** Platforms like Microsoft Copilot and others are moving into healthcare admin, HR, marketing, and operations right now. Understanding the idea before it lands in your workflow puts you ahead.
  - type: lesson
    title: What this looks like in your field
    body: |
      | Field / role | Bot version | Agent version |
      |---|---|---|
      | **Healthcare admin** | Auto-sends appointment reminders 24 hours before every visit. | Analyzes no-show patterns, drafts a re-engagement message for at-risk patients, and flags the list for a coordinator to review. |
      | **HR / recruiting** | Auto-replies to every application with a confirmation. | Reviews applications against criteria, scores candidates, drafts a shortlist summary, and schedules first-round interviews. |
      | **Marketing** | Posts approved content at a set time. | Monitors campaign performance, spots underperforming posts, rewrites copy variants, and A/B tests them. |
      | **Customer service** | Routes tickets tagged "billing" to the billing queue. | Reads the complaint, checks account history, drafts a resolution, and flags the case if it detects legal risk. |
      | **Operations / admin** | Sends a weekly report email every Friday at 9 AM. | Pulls data from three systems, identifies anomalies, writes a plain-English summary, and suggests two action items. |
- title: More autonomy, more responsibility
  intro: 'Here is the tension at the heart of AI agents: the same quality that makes them powerful, acting without step-by-step instructions, is what makes them risky.'
  blocks:
  - type: lesson
    title: Match your caution to the autonomy
    body: |
      A bot sends a wrong email once because of a bad rule. That is recoverable. An agent with access to your email, your calendar, and your customer records could take a long chain of actions before anyone notices something went sideways. The stakes are proportional to the autonomy.

      | What can go wrong with agents | What responsible use looks like |
      |---|---|
      | Takes a wrong action early, compounding errors across every later step. | Always review the final output. Do not assume that "finished" means "finished correctly." |
      | Misreads the goal because the prompt was vague. | Give agents specific, well-defined goals. RTCF still applies. |
      | Uses a tool in a way you did not expect: sends an email, deletes a file. | Start agents with read-only permissions. Add write access only after you trust the tool. |
      | Produces confident output that contains errors or invented facts. | Do not skip fact-checking just because it looks polished. Apply the same skepticism from "When AI Gets It Wrong." |
      | Takes actions affecting real people without a human review step. | Build a human-in-the-loop checkpoint for any agent action with outside impact. |

      > **Worth thinking about: the ethics questions agents raise.** When an agent makes a decision that harms someone, who is responsible: the person who ran it, the organization, or the developer? How do you audit a chain of AI decisions you did not personally make? What should always stay with a human, no matter how capable agents become? And how do you write policy for a tool that can act on its own?
- title: Practice and go deeper
  blocks:
  - type: activity
    title: Bot or agent? Classify and defend
    body: |
      Estimated time: 12 to 15 minutes.

      **Purpose:** to apply the bot-versus-agent distinction to real tools, and to think critically about what level of autonomy fits a given task.

      **Instructions:** for each scenario, decide whether it is a **bot (B)**, an **agent (A)**, or **mixed (M)**, and write one sentence explaining why.

      1. A tool that watches your inbox and files emails into folders based on keywords you set.
      2. A tool you give a research goal to. It searches the web, reads articles, and writes a three-paragraph summary.
      3. A chatbot on a retail website that answers product questions from a pre-built FAQ database.
      4. Microsoft Copilot in Word, which rewrites a selected paragraph when you click "rewrite," using the surrounding document as context.
      5. A system that monitors social media for brand mentions, identifies negative sentiment, drafts a response, and submits it for your approval before posting.
      6. A Zapier automation that sends you a Slack message every time a new row is added to a Google Sheet.

      **What to watch for:**

      - The "mixed" answer is not a cop-out. It is often the most accurate. Many real tools sit on a spectrum.
      - Scenario 4 is interesting: you triggered it, but it made decisions about word choice, tone, and structure. Where does your control end?
      - Scenario 5 is an agent, but with a human checkpoint built in. That checkpoint is the responsible design choice.
      - The hardest cases reveal the most. When you cannot easily classify something, that is a signal to ask more questions before trusting it.
